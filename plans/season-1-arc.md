# The Voyage: Season 1 Story Arc

## The question underneath

Season 1 asks whether a civilization should live by **prediction** or by **imagination**.

The saga so far has already answered half of it. The Tenretni were built from one maker's fear of loss: they archived everything, mined time to compute everything, and lost the ability to imagine anything new. The pilot's answer was curiosity. The light ship goes where its pilot imagines, and his drawing became the first thing the archive didn't already have.

This season puts that answer up against a rival that's winning. Out in the open universe, a civilization called the Foregone has turned the Tenretni's trick into an empire. They simulate the futures of whole worlds and sell the forecasts, and the worlds pay with their futures. Where the Tenretni feared losing the past, the Foregone fear surprise. The pilot and the light ship are the one thing their forecasts keep getting wrong.

The shape borrows from both shows. Most chapters stand alone the way TNG episodes do: a new world, a new people, a human question, a situation the crew has to understand before it can act. Underneath them runs a DS9-style serial: a hub you keep returning to, an economy, a cold war that turns hot at the midpoint, and a finale where winning means changing the enemy, not destroying it.

## How history works

The big events of the season happen no matter what you do. Your decisions change how they happen, who's there and what's left afterwards. The Foregone are right about *what* happens and wrong about *how*. That's the gap the pilot lives in.

| Fixed: it happens anyway | Yours: your choices bend it |
| --- | --- |
| The flare hits the dusk world (Ch 2) | Whether the night side's young are in its path |
| The ice ocean's people learn they aren't alone (Ch 3) | Whether they learn it from you, or later from someone who wants something |
| The Concourse floods with cheap ticks (Ch 6) | Whether the light ship is bound by a forecast going into the war |
| Ione defects (Ch 9) | Why she does it, and whether she trusts you |
| The siege comes, and the outer ring falls (Ch 10) | Who fights beside you, and who is still in the ring |
| Refugees need a home (Ch 11) | Which world they get, and what the archive loses |
| The black holes merge (Ch 13) | Whether the Foregone come out of it blind or curious |

Every fixed event shows on screen before it happens. The forecast is shown, so you always know what's coming, and the drama is in what you do with that. After each one, the Concourse's records and the crew's lines remember how it went in your save. That way the choices don't feel cosmetic even when the event itself was certain.

## The spine

Three things hold the season together. All the names are placeholders for you to rename.

| Element | What it is | Why it fits the game |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **The Concourse** | A free port at the stable L4 point of a binary star, where about a hundred species trade. The DS9 of the season: you come back to it between missions. A dockmaster, a market you walk, a bar, an embassy row, and a berth the light ship is too big for, so the freighter shuttles in. | The game already has walkable cities, governors and quest-givers. This one is a place that changes as the season goes. |
| **The tick** | The Concourse's currency: one second of banked proper time, mined by running compute deep in the gravity of a neutron star where clocks run slow. Prices, wages and debts are all in ticks. | It's the Tenretni's time mining turned into money. The light ship knows the technique better than anyone alive. |
| **The Foregone** | A civilization whose forecast engine simulates whole worlds' futures. They sell certainty: a world buys a forecast and pays with a contract on its future. Their envoys are calm and courteous and already know what you'll say. | They're the mirror image of the Tenretni. Fear of loss built the archive; fear of surprise built the engine. |

The economics run on real mechanics, so the money episodes have teeth.

- **Scarcity.** Ticks are expensive because deep-gravity mining is slow and dangerous.
- **Inflation.** When the Foregone start flooding the Concourse with cheap ticks from a bigger mine, prices climb and every saver gets poorer. That's the opening move of the war, before a shot is fired.
- **Futures.** A forecast is a futures contract. A world that sells its future gets paid now, and loses the right to be surprised later.
- **Labour value.** Vask keeps asking who actually did the work, and the answer changes how a trade looks.

The light ship carries the most valuable cargo in the known universe: an archive of 330 civilizations and the know-how to mine time. Everyone at the Concourse wants a piece of it. That pressure drives Part II.

## Crew arcs

Each captain's defining word from the game becomes their arc: Marrow sees, Lux fears, Vask counts. Over the season, each of those turns from a limit into a strength.

| Who | Starts as | Their chapter | Ends as |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **The pilot** | The one who imagines, but has never had to imagine under pressure | Ch 9, a love the Foregone forecast before it happened | Someone who chooses without knowing the outcome, and means it |
| **Cantor Ilse Marrow** (Saturnia, the bells) | Sees meaning everywhere, and trusts it too fast | Ch 7, a people whose faith is timed to a dying pulsar | Learns that seeing clearly includes seeing what you can't know |
| **Floor Manager Brandi Lux** (Wavemall Prime, Saturday) | Fear, plus a trader's instinct. The one most tempted to buy certainty | Ch 6, a world that sold its future and is happy | Fear becomes her gift: she's the one who knows when to run, and in the finale that saves the fleet |
| **Shift-Captain Orrin Vask** (Rustia, the mines) | Counts everything: time, cost, who did the work | Ch 8, machine builders still working for makers long gone | Counts the one thing the Foregone can't, the cost of never being surprised |

**If a captain was lost** in Entry Zero or past the horizon, their chapter still runs, carried by their quarters on the crew deck (kept, lights down, one left on) and by the archive. In Ch 11 the Concourse offers to recompile a lost captain from the archive's records. That's the Tenretni's old offer to rebuild your home world, now made about a person. Is a rebuilt friend really them? The pilot decides, and the decision is permanent. With no captains lost, Ch 11 asks the same question about a world instead.

## Part I: Open Space

Wonder first. Four new places and one marketplace, each chapter standing on its own, with the Foregone glimpsed only at the edges.

1. **Aftermath.** The crew takes stock of what the horizon cost and what it left aboard. The pilot stands at the helm chart, imagines somewhere, and for the first time the chart fills with a sky nobody has mapped.
    - Written only after you've played the sealed chapter, so it can answer what happened there (it reads `chapter.hz`).
    - You play: the bridge, the crew deck, the first passage.
    - Feel: grief and awe, in that order.
2. **The Long Dusk.** A tidally locked world around a red dwarf. One face always burns, the other is always frozen, and its people live in a ribbon of twilight between them, walking to keep pace with the light. Something on the night side hunts the edge of the ribbon, and the dusk people ask the crew for weapons. In infrared, the lamp shows the hunters are herding their young away from the route of a coming stellar flare. The "monsters" are evacuating.
    - Question: what do we assume about whoever lives in the dark?
    - You play: the freighter lands, you walk a moving terminator line, the lamp's infrared band shows what's warm out there.
    - Science: tidal locking, terminator habitability, red-dwarf flares.
    - Decision: arm the dusk people, or warn both sides and get the migration rerouted before the flare.
3. **Under the Ice.** A rogue planet, adrift with no star, with an ocean kept liquid by its own core under kilometres of ice. A people down there have never seen a sky and don't believe in one. They talk on the 21 cm radio band, the one the lamp only hears.
    - Question: do you tell a people the universe is bigger than they think?
    - You play: the submersible again, sound and radio over sight, first contact by tone.
    - Science: rogue planets, tidal and radiogenic heating, life without sunlight.
    - Decision: show them the sky (a hole through the ice, permanent), or leave them as they are and archive nothing. Marrow and Lux take opposite sides.
4. **The Concourse.** The free port at the binary's L4 point. You dock the freighter, walk the market, meet the dockmaster, learn the tick, and haul your first paid cargo. Lux is home in a way she hasn't been since Wavemall Prime. Then a Foregone envoy greets the pilot by name and orders his drink before he does.
    - Question: what's a fair price, and who sets it?
    - You play: a new trading loop. The freighter carries cargo between the Concourse and nearby systems, buying low and selling high in ticks, with prices that move.
    - Science: Lagrange points, gravitational time dilation as money.
5. **Appraisal.** The Concourse's assessors value the light ship's archive at more than the station itself. There are offers, then a theft attempt on the archive while the crew is ashore. Vask tracks the thieves by counting what's missing. The last scene: in the thieves' hold is a Foregone forecast, sealed four hundred years ago, describing the light ship's arrival, the crew by name, and the theft.
    - Question: is a culture property?
    - You play: a heist in reverse, walking the archive rooms on the light ship to find what was touched.
    - Hook: someone saw you coming centuries before you existed.

## Part II: The Forecast

The serial takes over. Each chapter is still a world of its own, but every world is now a move in a quiet war between the Foregone and the Concourse, and the light ship is the prize.

6. **Futures.** Lux's chapter. A world that sold its future to the Foregone generations ago. Every harvest, wedding and death arrives on schedule. Nobody is poor, nobody is afraid, and nothing has surprised anyone in three hundred years. Lux, who has been afraid her whole life, finds it beautiful. Meanwhile the Concourse is choking on cheap Foregone ticks, and prices there have doubled.
    - Question: what would you give up to never be afraid again?
    - You play: a perfect city on a perfect clock, where you can predict every citizen. Then you find the one person in it who is off schedule.
    - Decision: Lux can sign a forecast for the light ship. You can let her, or talk her down. Either way she stays.
7. **The Pulsar Choir.** Marrow's chapter. A civilization that times its prayers, its law and its music to a millisecond pulsar, the steadiest clock in nature. The pulsar has just glitched, a real and sudden spin-up, and their faith is breaking. The Foregone offer to forecast every future glitch in exchange for their allegiance.
    - Question: does faith need certainty?
    - You play: the lamp's radio band turns the pulsar into sound, and the cathedral-scale choir hall is tuned to it. Marrow, a Cantor, teaches them to hear the glitch as part of the music.
    - Science: pulsars, glitches, using pulsars as clocks.
    - Feel: the most awe-struck chapter of the season. It's the Emberdeep cathedral's spiritual successor.
8. **The Weight of Hands.** Vask's chapter. A half-built Dyson swarm around a dim star, still being assembled by machines whose makers vanished thousands of years ago. The Foregone want to buy the swarm's output to power their engine. The Concourse wants it to fight back. Nobody has asked the machines.
    - Question: when does a workforce become a people?
    - You play: the X-ray band shows what's inside the builders; a miner reads them. Vask counts shifts that have run for ten thousand years.
    - Science: Dyson swarms, the energy budget of a star, self-replicating machines.
    - Decision: free the builders (the swarm stops, and the Concourse loses the power it needed for the war), or contract their output to the Concourse.
9. **The Forecast of Love.** The pilot's chapter. The Foregone envoy from Ch 4 (placeholder name: Ione Vey) asks to see one of the archive's worlds. On the light ship's holodeck, at the ski hill on Wyattmattoe, she tells the pilot the engine forecast they would fall in love, and that she came anyway. If it was predicted, is it real? She answers it herself by doing the one thing her forecast says she won't: she stays aboard.
    - Question: is a choice still yours if someone saw it coming?
    - You play: the first chapter that's all conversation and place. A walk, a ski run, a meal in the dining lounge, the patio at night.
    - Love: real, unhurried, and expensive. Her defection is what starts the war.
10. **The Siege of the Concourse.** The midseason epic. A Foregone fleet arrives, and every one of its moves is already forecast. Concourse ships, plus ships from the worlds you helped (the Pulsar Choir, the swarm builders if you freed them), hold the line. The one thing the engine can't forecast is where the light ship goes, because it goes where the pilot imagines.
    - Question: what does courage look like against an enemy that knows what you'll do?
    - You play: a new battle mechanic. At the helm chart you **sketch** the light ship's next move freehand, the way you drew the first creature, and the ship flies the shape. The Foregone can't predict a drawing.
    - Battle: the biggest fleet the game has shown, bigger than the worldship war.
    - Cost: the Concourse's outer ring falls. Each decision earlier in the season decides who is in that ring, and some of them don't make it.

## Part III: What Kind of Civilization

The war's real question comes into the open. The crew learns why the Foregone exist, and winning stops meaning what it did.

11. **The Vault.** Thousands of refugees from the fallen outer ring need somewhere to live, and the Concourse can't pay for it. Two offers are on the table, and both come out of the archive. The Concourse will buy one archived world outright, enough ticks to house everyone. Or the light ship can recompile an archived world for the refugees, the way the Tenretni once offered to rebuild the pilot's home. If a captain was lost, the recompile offer is about them instead.
    - Question: is a rebuilt world, or a rebuilt friend, really the thing you lost?
    - You play: walk the holodeck program you might lose one last time.
    - Cost: permanent either way. A sold world leaves the holodeck for good. A recompiled one becomes real and leaves the archive, and you visit its people from then on.
12. **The Engine.** With Ione's help the crew reaches the forecast engine, built in orbit around a pair of black holes spiralling toward each other. Every orbit, the ship rings with the gravitational waves. Inside, the crew learns where the Foregone came from: a people wiped out by a catastrophe nobody saw coming, whose survivors swore never to be surprised again. The engine is their grief, the same as the megaworld was the Tenretni's.
    - Question: can you be at war with someone you understand?
    - You play: the lamp, all six bands, reading the oldest part of the engine, the way you read the first world at the centre of the megaworld.
    - Science: binary black holes, gravitational waves, the merger timescale. The engine is racing the merger.
    - Wonder: the waves are felt, not seen. The whole station stretches and squeezes.
13. **The Unforecast.** The finale battle, at the engine, with the binary minutes from merging. Each captain does what only they can: Marrow sees the pattern in the enemy's lines, Vask counts the seconds of the merger, Lux knows to the moment when to run. The pilot reaches the engine's core and makes the season's last choice.
    - Destroy it: the Foregone fleet goes blind, the war ends, and a people loses the only thing holding it together.
    - Feed it the archive: give the engine 330 civilizations' worth of imagination and the pilot's first drawing, more than it can forecast. It learns surprise, the way the Tenretni learned curiosity.
    - Ione's fate depends on the choice and on Ch 9.
    - The merger fires either way. Escaping it is the season's last set piece.
14. **Shore Leave.** The Concourse rebuilding. A new economy argued over at the bar: what's a tick worth when time can't be forecast? Dinner in the light ship's lounge with whoever made it. Ione, if she stayed. The holodeck with its gains and losses. Then the pilot at the helm chart, imagining, and the next blank sky.

## Build notes and open questions

New systems this season would add, all built on what's there:

| System | Built on | First chapter |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Freight trading in ticks, with prices that move | Inventory, the freighter as shuttle, `fgsf.voyage` | 4 |
| First contact by tone on the radio band | The lamp's 21 cm band, `entryzero-audio.js` | 3 |
| Sketching maneuvers at the helm chart | `drawPad.js` from the canvas beat | 10 |
| A living hub that changes as the season goes | City registry, governors, `capitalWalk` | 4 |
| Holodeck programs that can be permanently lost or made real | `ARCHIVE` and `ARCHIVE_LOCKED` in the home base | 11 |
| Fleet battle on the scale of the worldship war, bigger | `fleets.js`, `warships.js` | 10 |

The strongest argument against this plan is that a war serial fights what the game does best. So far the game is at its best alone: a cage drop, a cathedral, a garden in the sun, places you stand in and take in. A serial war needs politics, factions and dialogue, and the owner doesn't want stories told through reading. The plan guards against this by keeping every chapter a place first. Even so, Part II carries more talk than anything in the game, and Ch 9 is almost nothing but talk. If it starts to feel like reading, cut the Concourse politics before you cut the places.

Scope is the other risk. Fourteen chapters at Entry Zero's size is many months of builds. Build the standalones first (2, 3, 7, 8): each plays on its own, and together they test the trading, radio and sketching systems before the serial needs them. The Concourse (4) goes in next, since everything after depends on it.

On the science: the tidally locked world, the rogue ocean, the pulsar glitch, the Dyson swarm and the gravitational waves are all real. Two things are stretched. A stellar-mass black-hole pair spends only seconds in its final orbits, so a minutes-long finale means very large black holes or a lot of dramatic licence. And feeling gravitational waves with your body would need a distance where the tides would already kill you. Mark both in the code comments, the way the sealed chapter does.
